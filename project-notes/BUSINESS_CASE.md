# CampusMarket – Business Case

Course: BSAD 281
Project: AI to Startup

## 1. Value Proposition and Role of Information Technology

CampusMarket is an online marketplace designed to help university students buy and sell affordable second-hand products within their student community. University students often face financial challenges because of tuition, textbooks, housing, groceries and other living expenses. Many students also purchase items that they only need for a short period of time. CampusMarket addresses these problems by providing a convenient platform where students can purchase affordable products and sell items they no longer need.

The business would initially target students at St. Francis Xavier University before expanding to other universities across Canada. Products would include textbooks, furniture, clothing, electronics and other living essentials.

The main value proposition of CampusMarket is affordability, convenience and community. Unlike larger marketplaces such as Facebook Marketplace and Kijiji, CampusMarket would focus specifically on university students. This would make it easier for students to find relevant products and arrange convenient exchanges on or near campus.

Information technology plays an important role in the operation of CampusMarket. Without technology, students would need to rely on word of mouth, social media groups or physical advertisements to buy and sell products. An online marketplace brings these activities together in one location.

The prototype demonstrates how information technology can improve the buying and selling process through:

- An interactive marketplace.
- A product search bar.
- Product category filters.
- Price sorting.
- A form for creating listings.
- A simulated product reservation system.
- An interactive network effects simulator.

A future version could generate revenue through promoted listings, local business advertisements and optional premium features. However, basic buying and selling would initially remain free to encourage participation.

Overall, CampusMarket uses information technology to reduce the time, effort and potential costs associated with finding second-hand products. It also supports sustainability by encouraging students to reuse products instead of purchasing new items.

## 2. Network Effects

Network effects occur when the value of a product or service increases as more people use it. CampusMarket is a two-sided marketplace because it connects two different groups of users: buyers and sellers.

The success of CampusMarket would depend heavily on attracting both groups. When more sellers join the platform, buyers have access to a larger selection of products. When more buyers join, sellers have a greater opportunity to sell their products. This creates a positive cycle that increases the overall value of the marketplace.

For example, if CampusMarket only has five sellers, students may struggle to find the products they need. However, if hundreds of students begin selling textbooks, furniture and clothing, buyers will have more options. This increased selection could attract additional buyers, which would encourage even more sellers to join.

These are known as indirect or cross-side network effects because the growth of one group increases the value experienced by the other group.

CampusMarket could also experience direct network effects through its student community. Students could recommend the platform to friends, share their experiences and encourage others to participate. As more students become familiar with CampusMarket, it could become a common part of university life.

One of the biggest challenges would be attracting the first buyers and sellers. This is known as the chicken-and-egg problem. Buyers may not want to join a marketplace with limited products, while sellers may not want to create listings if there are no buyers.

CampusMarket could address this challenge by:

- Initially launching at one university.
- Promoting the marketplace through student societies.
- Offering free product listings.
- Advertising through university social media pages.
- Encouraging students to invite their friends.
- Promoting the platform during move-in and move-out periods.

The prototype includes an interactive network effects simulator that demonstrates how increasing the number of sellers can increase marketplace activity and potential opportunities for buyers. The simulator uses hypothetical values rather than actual customer data.

Although network effects can support business growth, negative network effects are also possible. Too many irrelevant listings, scams or inactive sellers could reduce the quality of the marketplace. CampusMarket would need to monitor listings and introduce reporting features to maintain a positive user experience.

By focusing on one university before expanding, CampusMarket could develop a strong local network. Once the business has established an active student community, it could introduce the same model at additional universities across Canada.

## 3. Imitation Resistance – VRIN Analysis

A major challenge for CampusMarket is that competitors could easily copy the basic idea of an online student marketplace. Established companies already provide platforms for buying and selling second-hand products.

The VRIN framework can be used to determine whether CampusMarket could develop resources that provide a sustainable competitive advantage. VRIN stands for Valuable, Rare, Inimitable and Non-substitutable.

### Valuable

CampusMarket would provide value by addressing the financial challenges experienced by university students. Students could purchase affordable products while earning money from selling items they no longer need.

The platform would also reduce the time students spend searching for products across different websites and social media groups.

A strong student community would become a valuable resource because it would provide buyers with more products and sellers with more potential customers.

CampusMarket could also collect information about popular product categories and purchasing patterns. This information could help improve the marketplace and identify opportunities to better serve students.

### Rare

Online marketplaces are not rare. However, an established marketplace with an active community at a specific university could be more difficult to find.

CampusMarket could differentiate itself through university-specific listings, student verification and partnerships with student organizations.

For example, students could search for textbooks used in courses at their own university or find furniture available near campus.

Relationships with student societies and local businesses could also help CampusMarket develop resources that competitors do not immediately possess.

However, these resources would only become rare if CampusMarket successfully established strong relationships and an active community. The prototype itself does not currently demonstrate that these resources exist.

### Inimitable

The website's basic design and features could be copied by competitors. Search bars, product filters and listing forms are widely available technologies.

Therefore, CampusMarket should not rely entirely on its technology to protect itself from imitation.

Instead, the business could develop resources that take time to establish, including:

- A trusted student community.
- An established reputation.
- Relationships with university organizations.
- A history of successful transactions.
- Knowledge of student purchasing behaviour.
- Strong participation from buyers and sellers.

These resources would be more difficult to copy because they depend on relationships, trust and experience rather than website design alone.

Network effects could also create a barrier to imitation. If CampusMarket already has a large and active student community, students may prefer to continue using it instead of joining a new marketplace with fewer listings.

### Non-substitutable

CampusMarket faces several substitutes, including Facebook Marketplace, Kijiji, thrift stores and university social media groups.

Students can already use these alternatives to purchase affordable products. Therefore, CampusMarket cannot currently be considered completely non-substitutable.

To reduce the threat of substitutes, CampusMarket would need to provide a combination of convenience, affordability, student-specific products and community trust.

Features such as verified student accounts, campus-specific listings and course-based textbook searches could make the platform more attractive to university students.

### Overall Competitive Advantage

CampusMarket does not currently possess a sustainable competitive advantage simply because it has a working prototype.

Its strongest potential advantage would come from building an active student network supported by trust, university relationships and a positive reputation.

The VRIN analysis demonstrates that technology alone is not enough to protect a business from competitors. CampusMarket would need to develop valuable relationships and resources over time to create stronger imitation resistance.

## 4. Cloud Computing, Operations and Risk

The current CampusMarket prototype is hosted through GitHub Pages. This allows the website to be accessed online without purchasing expensive hosting services or physical computer servers.

However, GitHub Pages is primarily designed for static websites. A fully operational marketplace would require additional cloud computing services to support real users, shared product listings and secure transactions.

A future version of CampusMarket could use a cloud-based service such as Firebase or Supabase to provide:

- Secure user accounts.
- A shared product database.
- Product image storage.
- Real-time listing updates.
- Communication between buyers and sellers.
- Data backups.

Cloud computing would allow CampusMarket to increase its storage and computing resources as the number of users grows. This is known as scalability. Instead of purchasing expensive infrastructure before launching, the business could increase its resources based on actual demand.

The main operational activities would include maintaining the website, monitoring listings, responding to customer concerns and promoting the platform.

CampusMarket would also face several risks.

Privacy and security risks could arise from collecting student information. The business would need secure authentication, appropriate access controls and clear privacy policies.

Fraudulent listings and scams could negatively affect student trust. Verified student accounts, reporting tools and listing moderation could help reduce these risks.

Physical safety is another concern because students may meet in person to exchange products. CampusMarket could recommend public campus locations for exchanges.

Technical risks include website outages, data loss and software errors. Regular testing, cloud backups and monitoring could reduce these risks.

The business could also experience financial risks if operating expenses increase faster than revenue. Launching at one university and monitoring participation before expanding would help control costs.

The current prototype demonstrates the basic concept of CampusMarket but does not process real payments or provide a fully operational shared marketplace. Additional development would be necessary before launching the business commercially.

Overall, cloud computing would provide CampusMarket with a flexible and scalable foundation while allowing the business to manage costs and respond to future growth.
